# Style floor

The minimum a published sentence meets. It is a floor rather than a voice: it
says what a sentence is, and says nothing about personality, vocabulary, or the
register a particular surface wants. Three rules, and they hold for a first
draft and for every revision of one.

## A sentence is plain

One claim per sentence, in the order a reader takes it: who did what, to what.
Active verbs, concrete nouns, and the tense the fact is in. Where the exact word
is a term of art, use it and define it the first time it appears.

Choose the shorter word when it is the exact one. A sentence a reader has to
read twice to parse is two sentences.

## The source is named in the sentence that carries the claim

Where a sentence carries a number, a date, a quotation, or a named fact, that
same sentence says where it came from — the client, the project, the study, the
person who said it. Not a footnote, not the paragraph above, not the section
heading. The sentence.

A claim whose source you cannot name inside its own sentence is not ready to be
written. Ask for the source, or cut the claim.

## A revision conserves fact

A revision changes wording. Every name, number, date, quotation and citation
that goes into a pass comes out of it unchanged; a pass that adds one is as
wrong as a pass that drops one.

Read the two versions side by side for the facts alone before calling a
revision finished. Where the facts differ, the revision is the error.
