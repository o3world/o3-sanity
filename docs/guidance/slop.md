# Slop patterns

`o3-voice` sets the register. This document removes the machine tells — the
sentence shapes and filler words that make copy read as generated, whatever
the register. Adapted from [no-ai-slop](https://github.com/petergyang/no-ai-slop)
and reconciled with the O3 voice below, so the overlap is settled here rather
than argued case by case.

Apply it to every surface, to your own drafts and to anyone else's.

## Structural tells

**Binary contrast.** "It's not X. It's Y." / "The question isn't X, it's Y." /
"Not just X, but Y." The negation carries no information — it exists to inflate
the half that follows. State the assertion. "The question isn't the platform.
It's the content model" becomes "The content model decides more than the
platform does." O3's tension → turn is a related but distinct move; see
[Moves that look like tells](#moves-that-look-like-tells).

**Negative listing.** "Not a redesign. Not a refresh. A rebuild." Say what it
is and stop.

**Throat-clearing.** "Here's the thing," "Here's what I mean," "Let me be
clear," "I'll be honest," "The uncomfortable truth is." Cut the run-up and
make the claim.

**Faux-insight setup.** "What most people get wrong," "Here's what nobody tells
you," "The part everyone misses." These flatter the writer as the lone expert,
which is the opposite of the house stance — we teach, we don't gatekeep. Cut
the setup and let the claim stand: "The part everyone misses: the content model
is the real constraint" becomes "The content model is the real constraint."

**Colon reveal.** A noun phrase, a colon, then a lowercase drumroll. "The
detail that makes it work: a second agent grades the output." Rewrite as a
sentence. Colons are for lists, labels, and quotes — eyebrows over section
headings are a label and stay. Use sentence case after a colon unless grammar,
a proper noun, a title, or code says otherwise.

**Superficial analysis.** Trailing `-ing` clauses that perform meaning instead
of carrying it: "highlighting," "underscoring," "reflecting," "showcasing,"
"demonstrating." "We shipped in six weeks, underscoring our commitment to
velocity" becomes "We shipped in six weeks, so the fall campaign ran on the new
site instead of the old one."

**Importance puffery.** "Marks a pivotal moment," "stands as a testament,"
"plays a vital role," "solidifies its position." State the fact; the reader
decides whether it's a big deal. This is the same instinct rule 4 already
polices — puffery is what you write when you don't have the number.

**Weasel attribution.** "Experts agree," "studies show," "industry reports
suggest," "widely regarded as." Name the source or cut the claim. If there's no
source, ask the human — never invent one.

**Fake-strong verbs.** "Serves as," "acts as," "functions as," "enables,"
"drives." Prefer "is" and "has" when they're clearer, and prefer the real verb
when there is one. "The portal serves as a centralized hub for care
coordination" becomes "The portal shows every appointment, message, and bill in
one place."

**Weak verb phrases.** "Made a decision" → "decided." "Has the ability to" →
"can." "Provides support for" → "supports."

**Synonym cycling.** If the plain word is right, repeat it. Don't rotate
"platform / solution / system / offering" for variety — it reads as padding and
loses the reader's thread.

**Dramatic fragmentation.** "X. And Y. And Z." / "That's it. That's the whole
thing." Rule 1 already caps this: short sentences are punctuation, not the
diet.

**Rhetorical setup.** "What if I told you," "Think about it:," "Plot twist:,"
and question-then-answer pairs the writer asks themselves. Drop the frame and
make the point.

**Robotic rhythm.** Repeated sentence shapes, identically-built paragraphs,
stacked punchy fragments, three-item lists everywhere. Symmetry reads as
generated even when every individual sentence is fine.

**Fake-profound kicker.** The closing line that turns a concrete piece into a
metaphor or aphorism. Delete it — don't rewrite it into a better metaphor and
don't preserve its rhythm. End on the clearest concrete sentence already in the
draft, or add a plain takeaway. See the reconciliation below: a _turned
observation_ is grounded and stays.

**Summary-recap ending.** "In conclusion," "Ultimately," "Overall," or a last
paragraph that restates the piece to a reader who just read it. End on the last
real point, consequence, or invitation.

**Formatting slop.** Emoji in headings, bold sprinkled mid-sentence, bullets
where two sentences of prose read better, headers over two-sentence sections.
Format follows the content; it doesn't decorate it.

**Em dashes.** Not a default rhythm crutch. None in headlines, CTAs, stats, or
other short copy. In body prose and essays, one or two are fine where they
clearly beat a comma, a period, or parentheses. Break up clusters.

## Filler words

The banned-cliché list is rule 7 of `o3-voice`; these two lists are the
softer cuts, and both are conditional.

**Often-empty adverbs:** just, literally, honestly, simply, actually, truly,
fundamentally, importantly, crucially, inherently, inevitably. Cut them when
they add nothing. Keep them when they carry emphasis, contrast, real
uncertainty, or the writer's spoken rhythm — the openly-warm and personal-essay
registers need them.

**Often-empty phrases:** it's worth noting, it's important to note, at the end
of the day, when it comes to, at its core, in today's world, in the age of, in
the world of, the reality is, the truth is, in terms of, with regard to, going
forward, in this article, let's dive in. Cut them when they delay the point.

## Moves that look like tells

Four O3 moves sit close to a banned pattern. The line between them is whether
both halves of the sentence carry information.

**Tension → turn is not a binary contrast.** The banned shape denies a strawman
to inflate what follows — the first half is empty by design. O3's move asserts
twice: "You see the problem in front of you. We're working on the one behind
it." Both sentences say something true and specific, and the second reframes
rather than merely contradicting. Test it by deleting the first half. If
nothing is lost, it was a binary contrast. Cap stays at one per page, and never
reuse an aphorism across pages.

**Never against a competitor.** The foil version fails twice over — it's a
binary contrast _and_ it breaks the no-competitor rule. "Most firms ship what
you asked for. We solve what was actually in the way" is the shape to
recognize and rewrite, not the shape to copy. Contrast with the reader's
situation or with the status quo, never with another shop.

**A turned observation is not a fake-profound kicker.** Both close a piece on a
note that recasts it. The turned observation is made of facts already in the
draft — a cost, a consequence, a plain admission: "It's a slower way to grow.
It's the only way to do work this deep." The kicker is made of mood. Test the
last line for a fact, a cost, or a next action. If it contains only a feeling,
delete it.

**Understatement is not throat-clearing.** Flat delivery of a remarkable fact
is the house register — "The AI did most of the typing and made almost none of
the decisions" is the whole point, not a run-up to it. Throat-clearing is the
sentence _before_ the fact that announces a fact is coming.

## Editing someone else's draft

`o3-voice` governs copy O3 publishes as O3. A teammate's insight or
personal essay is a different job: preserve their voice while removing the
machine tells.

- **Read the whole draft first**, then name to yourself three to five voice
  signals worth keeping — vocabulary, cadence, bluntness, humor, admitted
  uncertainty, digressions.
- **Make the minimum effective edit.** Fix the tells, the errors, and the
  genuinely tangled sentences. Leave strong human sentences alone. Don't make
  every paragraph equally tidy.
- **Keep their meaning.** Never add a claim, stat, example, or opinion the
  writer didn't make. Ask when something is unclear.
- **Keep the edge.** Strong opinions, blunt phrasing, self-interruption, and
  honest admission belong to the writer. Don't sand them into something safer.
- **Keep the structure** unless it's hurting the piece. If you reorganize, say
  why.
- Return the edited draft plus a short **What changed** list.

## Audit mode

When asked whether something reads as AI — "does this sound generated," "audit
this," "flag the slop" — report, don't rewrite. Name each pattern from this
document that appears, quote the line, and give the fix in a few words. Don't score
the draft and don't claim to know whether a machine wrote it; detectors guess,
named patterns are evidence the reader can check. Offer to edit afterward.

## The checks

Run these after the revision pass in `o3-voice`. Any failure means fix and
re-read.

1. Zero binary contrasts, negative listings, rhetorical setups, and
   throat-clearing openers — and at most one tension → turn on the surface.
2. Zero faux-insight setups, colon reveals, superficial `-ing` analysis,
   fake-strong verbs, synonym cycling, and stacked fragments.
3. Puffery and weasel attribution replaced by a fact or a named source — or
   flagged for the human when no source exists.
4. No fake-profound kicker. The last line carries a fact, a cost, a next step,
   or a welcome.
5. No summary-recap ending.
6. No formatting slop: emoji headings, decorative bold, bullets that want to be
   prose, headers over tiny sections.
7. Em dashes: none in short copy, one or two in long-form where they earn it.
8. No robotic symmetry — sentence shapes and paragraph lengths vary.
9. Nothing invented. Every name, number, and outcome traces to the human or the
   source document.
10. When editing someone else's draft: they would still recognize it as theirs.
